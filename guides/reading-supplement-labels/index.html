How to read a supplement label in two minutes

You do not need a biochemistry degree. You need to know where companies hide things, and there are only a few places.

By Ernesto LucianoOctober 20266 minute read

The front of a supplement bottle is advertising. The back is closer to a legal document, and it is where the actual information lives. Here is the order worth reading it in.

1. Check the serving size first, not the ingredients

Every quantity on the panel refers to one serving. If the serving is three capsules and the bottle holds ninety, that is a thirty-day supply, not ninety. This quietly triples the real price of a product that looked cheaper than its competitor. Do this arithmetic before anything else, because it sometimes ends the decision on its own.

2. Look for the words "proprietary blend"

A proprietary blend lists several ingredients and one combined weight. You learn the total but not the split, which means a blend advertised at 500 mg could be 480 mg of the cheapest component and 20 mg spread across the ones named in the marketing.

It is legal and it is sometimes genuinely about protecting a formula. It also makes the dose impossible to check, and you should price that uncertainty in.

The useful question. Not "is this a proprietary blend?" but "can I tell how much of the ingredient they advertise is actually in here?" If the answer is no, every claim resting on that ingredient is unverifiable by design.

3. For probiotics, read the CFU count and then ignore it

CFU means colony-forming units — roughly, how many live organisms are in a serving. Two things make the number less useful than it looks.

First, more is not better in any simple way. The relevant question is whether these strains do anything for this outcome, not how many of them there are. A large count of irrelevant strains is still irrelevant.

Second, and more important: look for when the count is guaranteed. "At time of manufacture" means the company counted on the day it was made and makes no promise about what survived shipping, shelf time and your kitchen cupboard. "At expiry" or "through end of shelf life" is a meaningfully stronger commitment. Many labels do not say, which is itself an answer.

4. Named strains beat ingredient names

"Lactobacillus reuteri" names a species containing many strains that behave differently. "L. reuteri DSM 17938" names one specific, identifiable organism that can be traced to specific research. The code after the name is the useful part. Its absence means you cannot connect the label to any particular study.

5. Find the refund terms before you find the price

For any product without published evidence, the guarantee is the evidence you are buying. A sixty-day window where you can return opened product changes the real cost of trying something from the price of the bottle to the cost of your time. A fourteen-day window on unopened product only is close to no guarantee at all for something you are meant to take daily.

Read who handles the refund, too. For products sold through a payment platform rather than the manufacturer directly, the platform usually processes returns — which is often easier, not harder.

What is not on the label

Third-party testing, the manufacturing facility, and whether anyone independent has verified the contents are usually absent. Their absence is not proof of a problem. It is simply unknown, and unknown is where most of this category sits.
